November 7, 1944: in Tokyo’s Sugamo Prison, Japan hanged Richard Sorge, the man Ian Fleming called “the most formidable spy in history.”

Sorge posed as a loyal German journalist, writing for the Frankfurter Zeitung and even joining the Nazi Party. He grew so close to Germany’s man in Tokyo, Eugen Ott, that he was given his own desk at the German embassy. All the while he was running a Soviet military intelligence network across Japan.

In 1941 he warned Moscow that Germany was preparing to invade the Soviet Union. Stalin scoffed. That September, Sorge reported that Japan would not attack the USSR, welcome news for Stalin as German armies closed in on Moscow.

Japanese police arrested him in October 1941. He was hanged on the anniversary of the Russian Revolution, the same day as his Japanese collaborator Hotsumi Ozaki. Twenty years later, the Soviet Union declared him a Hero of the Soviet Union.
