November 2, 1944: the U.S. 28th Infantry Division stepped off into the Hürtgen Forest. It would become one of the costliest attacks any American division made in the war.

The Hürtgen was a dark, dense fir forest on Germany’s western border, laced with bunkers, minefields and barbed wire. Tanks could barely move on its muddy trails. German shells burst in the treetops and rained steel and wood splinters on the men below, so there was almost nowhere safe to take cover.

That morning the 28th, Pennsylvania’s National Guard division, attacked toward the villages of Vossenack and Schmidt. They took Schmidt the next day, lost it to a German counterattack, and spent the days that followed clinging to the ridges under relentless fire.

The Battle of Hürtgen Forest dragged on for 88 days, the longest battle fought on German soil in World War II. It cost the U.S. First Army at least 33,000 men killed and wounded.
